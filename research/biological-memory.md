# Biological memory: mechanisms, limits, and functional analogies

Biological memory offers useful questions for agent design: how to preserve distinct experiences, integrate recurring structure, retrieve from incomplete cues, and revise knowledge without confusing its origins. It does not supply a ready-made filesystem architecture. This structured scoping review covers **22 public primary scholarly sources**, including original experiments, explicitly labeled reviews, and a computational theory paper. Six studies are from 2024–2026. Selection follows the mechanisms relevant to retention and fresh-agent onboarding; it is not an exhaustive systematic review.

The cutoff is **2026-10-08 UTC**. The [catalog](../sources/biological-memory.json) records species or model, publication status, primary links, and actual inspection depth. Some papers were inspected through selected full-text sections or figures; others through abstracts and indexed primary-text excerpts when access was restricted. No experiments were reproduced. Human behavior, clinical recordings, animal interventions, and computational simulations are distinguished below. Engineering proposals are labeled inferences.

## Multiple systems, different learning problems

**Squire and Dede’s review** distinguishes declarative memory—facts and context-bound episodes—from nondeclarative abilities such as skills, habits, priming, and conditioning. Human amnesia and animal research motivate systems with different operating principles, rather than a single memory faculty. These categories do not imply isolated containers: the review also emphasizes that task strategies can differ across species. Knowing how to perform something and recalling its learning episode are different outcomes. [BM01](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4355270/)

**McClelland, McNaughton, and O’Reilly’s complementary-learning account** proposes rapid hippocampal learning alongside slower, interleaved learning of shared structure in neocortex. Connectionist analyses explain a tension between incorporating particular experiences and changing overlapping representations without catastrophic interference. This is a computationally motivated theory interpreted against biological evidence, not a demonstration that all cortical learning is slow or that every remote memory becomes hippocampus-independent. [BM02](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMcNaughtonOReilly95.pdf)

**Thompson and colleagues’ 2026 mouse study** provides a recent boundary: striatal sleep replay associated with a procedural task persisted after bilateral hippocampal lesions. Post-training striatal NMDA-receptor blockade impaired later performance. The lesions support hippocampal nonnecessity for this task; the drug affects broader plasticity processes. Because replay itself was not selectively disrupted, its causal contribution remains unresolved. One hippocampal replay model cannot cover all learning systems. [BM20](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02362-5)

**Interpretation.** An agent’s task history, accepted facts, reusable procedures, and current working plan answer different questions. Treating them as interchangeable “memories” hides different correctness conditions. A procedure should be checked through execution; a fact through supporting evidence; a historical episode through source and time. Keeping these roles explicit is a functional design choice. A prompt’s finite input size is not a measurement of biological working memory, and an embedding index is not a hippocampus. The review supports examining learning and retrieval problems, not assigning brain-region names to folders.

## Binding an episode and retrieving it from partial cues

**Teyler and Rudy’s indexing-theory review** proposes that hippocampal activity binds the distributed cortical activity associated with an episode. A partial cue can reactivate that index and help reinstate the broader cortical pattern. The account explains associative access rather than a literal copy stored at one location. It remains a theoretical synthesis: its terminology does not establish that every feature of an experience is retained or that reinstatement is always accurate. [BM03](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17696170/)

**Nakazawa and colleagues** genetically removed NMDA receptors from hippocampal CA3 pyramidal cells in mice. Spatial performance and CA1 activity were relatively preserved with full environmental cues but impaired after partial cue removal, supporting a role in associative pattern completion. The authors discuss a crucial qualification: impaired receptor-dependent formation of the representation could later appear as a recall deficit. The manipulation does not isolate a receptor’s role only during retrieval. [BM04](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2877140/)

**Kerrén and colleagues’ 2026 human study** recorded intracranial activity in epilepsy patients during associative retrieval. Successful retrieval showed hippocampal-ripple-linked cortical reinstatement and increased representational dimensionality, with controls for timing and epileptiform activity. The observations fit an indexing or decompression account. They do not directly demonstrate compressed hippocampal encoding or prove that ripples caused the cortical changes; clinical electrode placement also limits coverage. [BM21](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75345-6)

**Interpretation.** A retrieval failure can arise because relevant information was never encoded, the available cue reaches the wrong association, or the downstream system fails to use recovered evidence. These explanations require different checks. For files, the distinction is unusually inspectable: one can examine the original artifact, an extracted representation, search results, and the agent’s subsequent actions. Associative access is a reason to test multiple routes to evidence—entities, tasks, dates, decisions, and dependencies—while retaining exact identifiers when they are available. It is not a reason to replace deterministic lookup with similarity search.

## Schemas, gist, and the persistence of detail

**Tse and colleagues’ rat experiment** found that established flavor–place associative knowledge could support rapid incorporation and consolidation of new associations. Their “schema” is an experimentally trained structure, inferred through task behavior and hippocampal dependence. This qualifies an unconditional slow-cortical-learning story. It does not show that arbitrary new information assimilates rapidly, nor that a human semantic schema is equivalent to a learned spatial task structure. [BM06](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17412951/)

**Winocur, Moscovitch, and Bontempi’s transformation review** argues that a contextual, detailed memory and a more schematic cortical representation can coexist and change their contributions at retrieval. It challenges a simple account in which consolidation duplicates an unchanged memory elsewhere and removes hippocampal dependence. This is a competing theoretical interpretation of human and animal findings, rather than a settled rule that all memories lose their detail with age. [BM07](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20430044/)

**Ko and colleagues’ 2025 mouse study** links adult neurogenesis to reorganization of hippocampal engram circuitry and contextual or spatial memory resolution. Suppression, promotion, newborn-neuron interventions, and engram/projection manipulations provide converging evidence beyond running or irradiation alone. The reviewed abstract and extended-data captions support this circuit-level account. Behavioral generalization is not a direct assay of human semantic knowledge, and these interventions do not isolate replay. [BM18](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08993-1)

**Interpretation.** Generalization and precision should be evaluated separately. A concise project summary can expose recurring structure and make unfamiliar work understandable, while an exact artifact can answer a narrow question that the summary omitted. A system designed for complete important context should retain both and show the dependency between them. The biological debate cautions against assuming that a consolidated summary is an equivalent substitute for the source. New evidence may fit an existing organizational schema, expose its limits, or require a new distinction. A schema useful for onboarding can also make an unusual exception easy to overlook.

## Event boundaries and retrieval context

**Baldassano and colleagues’ human narrative study** used fMRI and a data-driven event model to identify relatively stable activity patterns at different timescales. Boundaries in higher-order regions were associated with hippocampal activity and later reinstatement during recall. This supports hierarchical event organization in the tested narratives. Correlations and model-derived boundaries do not prove that imposing the same segmentation on another task will improve memory. [BM08](https://www.dpmlab.org/papers/Neuron17.pdf)

**Murre’s 2021 human replication** tested divers learning and recalling word lists on land and underwater, but did not reproduce a same-environment recall advantage. The paper documents differences in pool setting, timing, presentation, and possible interference from conducting conditions close together. This result does not refute every context-cue effect; it shows why a familiar environmental-cue example should not become an unconditional design law. [BM09](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8568063/)

**Rasch and colleagues’ human experiment** reintroduced a learning-associated odor during sleep. Declarative retention improved with cueing during slow-wave sleep, with different results for procedural material, other states, and odor absent during learning. This is a controlled, cue- and state-specific intervention. It supports a role for reactivation under those conditions, not a general claim that any repeated cue or any period without activity improves every memory. [BM11](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17347444/)

**Interpretation.** Task transitions are candidate organizational boundaries: a new goal, accepted decision, experiment, or correction may deserve a discoverable record. Clock time alone is another candidate, not the definition of an episode. Retrieval metadata should help reconstruct the relevant context without making the original interaction environment a prerequisite. A new agent should be able to find why a decision governed a particular task even when it uses a different tool or begins from a different entry point. The appropriate boundary is therefore an empirical question about retrieval and continuation, not a universal chunk size.

## Replay, consolidation, and selective prioritization

**Girardeau and colleagues’ rat experiment** disrupted detected hippocampal sharp-wave ripples during post-training rest and sleep and impaired spatial learning, compared with delayed-stimulation and unimplanted controls. Sleep architecture remained relatively preserved. This provides causal evidence for the disrupted activity under that task. However, stimulation also suppressed associated hippocampal discharge; it did not selectively remove only a particular replay sequence or isolate every mechanism of consolidation. [BM10](https://buzsakilab.com/content/PDFs/Girardeau2009NatNeurosci.pdf)

**Yang and colleagues’ 2024 mouse study** links waking sharp-wave-ripple content with preferential replay of the same trial blocks during subsequent sleep. Decoder and shuffled/simulated controls support selective reactivation rather than uniform replay of everything experienced. The evidence is observational: selective replay was not perturbed, and trial-specific long-term behavioral retention was not measured. “Selection for memory” is therefore stronger as a proposed mechanism than as a directly demonstrated retention outcome. [BM17](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.adk8261)

**Widloski and Foster’s 2025 rat study** detected spatial sequences independently of ripples and found replay without detectable sharp-wave ripples. Multiple detection controls strengthen the result, while thresholds, recording coverage, and excluded stationary replay bound what “without” means. It does not establish functional necessity. A 2026 [correction notice](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-72252-8) specifies amended references. Replay and a particular oscillatory marker should not be treated as interchangeable. [BM19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-65181-5)

**Anderson, Bjork, and Bjork’s human experiments** found that practicing retrieval of some category members impaired later recall of related unpracticed members, including conditions controlling output order. The result supports retrieval-induced forgetting and motivates a suppression account. It does not demonstrate destruction of representations, nor establish that the forgetting was beneficial in every task; competing access and interference explanations require their own evidence. [BM12](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/07/Anderson_RBjork_EBjork_1994.pdf)

**Interpretation.** Repeated access can change which information becomes easy to retrieve, and prioritization can create blind spots. An agent index that favors recent or frequently used records could similarly underexpose a rare but governing constraint; the software mechanism would need to be measured directly. Biological forgetting supplies no authority to delete evidence. Offline maintenance is a hypothesis about improving organization, source coverage, or derived views. Calling it “sleep” adds no validation. Useful tests should identify what the maintenance step changes, whether important exceptions remain reachable, and whether the next agent receives a fresh accepted-state view.

## Reconstruction, provenance, and updating

**Davachi, Mitchell, and Wagner’s human fMRI study** distinguished later item recognition from recollection of the item’s encoding context. Hippocampal and posterior parahippocampal encoding activity predicted source recollection, while perirhinal activity predicted item recognition. This supports separable contributions to item and contextual memory in the tested task. Subsequent-memory correlations do not establish exclusive regional functions or prove causal binding; remembering content remains distinct from recovering its source. [BM13](https://web.mit.edu/wagner/www/papers/DAV_PNAS03.pdf)

**Roediger and McDermott’s human list-learning experiments** elicited false recall and confident recognition of unpresented words strongly associated with studied words. Retrieval enhanced later remembering of both presented and unpresented material. This demonstrates reconstructive errors in a controlled semantic-association paradigm. It does not quantify the reliability of ordinary autobiographical memory or identify a single neural mechanism responsible for every false recollection. [BM16](https://psychnet.wustl.edu/memory/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Roediger-McDermott-1995_JEPLMC.pdf)

**Nader, Schafe, and LeDoux’s rat fear-conditioning study** found later amnesia after amygdala protein-synthesis inhibition shortly following memory reactivation; no-reactivation and delayed-treatment conditions constrained the effect. It supports retrieval-triggered lability and reconsolidation under this protocol. Pharmacological amnesia is not, by itself, proof of irreversible erasure, and the result does not mean every retrieval destabilizes every memory. [BM14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10963596/)

**Hupbach and colleagues’ human experiments** found that a reminder before learning a second object list increased later incorporation of second-list objects into recall of the first. The effect emerged after a delay and depended on the reminder; original-list recall was not simply reduced. The authors interpret this as updating through reconsolidation. Behavioral intrusions alone do not isolate the cellular mechanism from contextual or source-attribution explanations. [BM15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1838545/)

**Interpretation.** Generated summaries can blend observations, inferred relationships, and later corrections into fluent text. For software, provenance can be stored explicitly rather than inferred from familiarity: a claim should point to its supporting artifact and distinguish quoted evidence, extraction, hypothesis, and accepted decision. Updating a claim should identify what changed and why. This is an engineering response to an analogous error problem, not a claim that digital metadata reproduces biological source monitoring. Repeatedly retrieving a mistaken summary should not convert it into stronger evidence.

## Recall failure does not uniquely locate information loss

**Ryan and colleagues’ 2015 mouse study** found that optogenetic activation of tagged engram cells could elicit conditioned behavior despite protein-synthesis-inhibitor-induced natural-cue amnesia. Retained engram connectivity supported the authors’ storage-versus-access interpretation. The intervention provides a different route to expressing learned behavior; it does not demonstrate that every feature of the episode survives, nor that all causes of amnesia preserve an accessible trace. [BM05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26023136/)

**Kim, Hong, and Kaang’s 2026 mouse Micro Report** found that a stronger protein-synthesis-inhibitor combination impaired both natural and artificial recall, whereas anisomycin alone spared optogenetic recall in the tested pathway. This bounds a universal rescue claim. Broad drugs and a different circuit prevent a clean contradiction of every earlier result. The mechanism was not directly investigated, and failed artificial recall still cannot uniquely distinguish erased information from disrupted access or behavioral expression. [BM22](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13041-026-01305-2)

**Interpretation.** “The agent forgot” is similarly an outcome, not a diagnosis. A public evaluation should distinguish absent source material, omitted extraction, stale state, an inaccessible index, misleading retrieval, and failure to use evidence already present. Source inspection can establish byte preservation, while retrieval and resumed execution establish different capabilities. Neither a retained file nor a successful answer to one cue proves complete operational context retention. Testing several cues and downstream actions makes the failure claim more informative.

## Testable functional analogies and counterexamples

These proposals follow from the functional problems above. None requires reproducing brain anatomy or physiology. They are candidates for public filesystem experiments, with accuracy and preservation of important context as primary outcomes.

| Biological motif | Engineering hypothesis | Counterexample or failure to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distinct learning and memory roles | Keep evidence, accepted state, procedures, and open plans distinguishable and cross-linked. | Extra separation fragments a dependency that a single well-organized artifact preserved. |
| Associative indexing | Offer several navigational routes to the same original evidence. | Similar entities or repeated vocabulary retrieve the wrong project or revision. |
| Detailed and schematic representations | Use an onboarding summary with explicit links to exact supporting records. | The summary improves broad understanding but hides a rare governing exception. |
| Event organization and context cues | Index meaningful task transitions and preserve their causal context. | A boundary splits the decision from its evidence, or unfamiliar terminology defeats retrieval. |
| Selective reactivation | Periodically audit high-value constraints and neglected evidence for reachability and consistency. | Auditing reinforces frequently encountered errors or leaves rare constraints undiscovered. |
| Source monitoring and updating | Preserve claim provenance and explicit supersession relationships. | An inference is promoted to fact, or a later timestamp incorrectly overrides authority. |

Compare each proposal with a simple source-preserving baseline using the same public corpus. Include current-state questions, historical explanations, incomplete cues, and a fresh agent continuing a bounded task. Record evidence exposure and errors separately from final success. An improvement on paraphrased recall with worse revision handling is a tradeoff, not an unqualified improvement in memory.

Counterexamples should be part of the design brief. An exact identifier can outperform associative retrieval; a small complete artifact can outperform layers of summaries; an authoritative one-time correction can matter more than repeated observations. Digital evidence can remain immutable while accepted interpretations evolve. Human or animal adaptations to limited biological resources need not be optimal for a system whose purpose is accurate, auditable retention. The strongest transfer is a falsifiable account of which functional problem an organizational mechanism solves, and which important context it might make harder to recover.
