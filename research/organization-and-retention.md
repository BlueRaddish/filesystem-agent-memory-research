# Organization and retention: adjacent evidence for filesystem agent memory

Search cutoff: **2026-10-08 UTC**. This structured scoping review asks what personal information management (PIM), creator-developed organizational practices, and digital preservation can contribute to filesystem agent memory. It does not establish that a filesystem, a named folder method, or greater retention improves agent accuracy.

## Scope and evidence handling

The search combined named foundational studies with targeted queries for folders, tags, search, facets, project context, demotion, retention, provenance, and preservation standards. Separate 2025/2026 queries sought recent adjacent work. Inclusion required a public primary research publication, an author or institutional research record, creator documentation, or an authoritative specification. Citation chaining connected the human studies to subsequent guidance. This is a purposive scoping review, not an exhaustive systematic review: there was no preregistered protocol, database-wide screening count, quality scoring, or quantitative pooling. Search engines' crawl dates were not treated as publication dates.

The companion [source catalog](../sources/organization-retention.json) records 21 sources, access dates, review depth, and limitations. Published studies, creator testimony, editorials, specifications, and this review's engineering inferences remain distinct. Some full texts were inspected selectively; three empirical studies are summarized only from accessible abstracts or author summaries. No copyrighted source full text is reproduced in the repository. The undated Johnny.Decimal documentation uses the consulted version's year in the catalog, explicitly identified as such.

The target question is whether retained information can be found, interpreted, scoped, and used correctly in a later task. Physical survival, successful lookup, historical authenticity, present applicability, and accurate generation are different outcomes. An item can satisfy several and fail another.

## What the human studies actually show

**Strategies vary across tools and over time.** Boardman and Sasse examined files, email, and bookmarks for 31 experienced computer users; eight continued in a longitudinal phase. Organization differed across the same person's collections, and participants reported preferring browsing to search. Maintenance often received attention around major changes rather than continuously. The sample was largely university-affiliated, with dated applications and substantial self-report, so neither the prevalence of strategies nor their optimality should be generalized to present agent systems. The result challenges the assumption that one uniform scheme will suit every information type. [OR01](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/13438/)

**Keeping serves more than retrieval.** Bruce, Jones, and Dumais studied keeping and re-finding Web information through workplace observation/interviews, delayed recall, and a 214-person survey. Keeping methods provided different combinations of reminding, context, access, and integration; none covered every desired function. Delayed tests also observed successful re-finding through routes requiring no explicit saved bookmark, including search and remembered routes. These were cued returns to previously encountered sites, not unrestricted discovery or durable recovery from disappearing pages. The important distinction is between making an item available and preserving the reason to revisit it. [OR02](https://informationr.net/ir/10-1/paper207.html)

**Project, importance, and context are meaningful organizing attributes.** Bergman and colleagues' 2008 study combined interviews, questionnaires, and computer screen captures. Participants commonly described information through projects, while application boundaries fragmented project-related material by format. They also discussed subjective importance and context. The paper's proposed interfaces were design implications requiring further testing, not validated products. Much of its evidence was reported behavior. This supports studying task-based organization and the role of an item; it does not validate a particular directory tree or prove that project grouping reduces hallucinations. [OR03](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/asi.20738)

**Multiple classification does not automatically outperform folders.** The accessible abstract of Bergman and colleagues' folder-versus-tag study describes 75 Gmail participants and 23 Windows 7 participants. Both studies found folder preference; participants often used only one tag, and the controlled retrieval task favored folders in success and speed. These findings are bounded to the studied interfaces, training, tasks, and human familiarity. The full methods and statistical analysis were not reviewed here. Free-form tagging, supervised metadata, and modern semantic search should not be treated as the same intervention. [OR04](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/asi.22906)

**Facets offer a different alternative to a single hierarchy.** Yee and colleagues compared a faceted metadata interface with a conventional search interface using 32 art history students and about 35,000 fine arts images. Ninety percent preferred the faceted approach overall. The interface combined browsing with keyword search and visible query refinements. This is evidence about an image collection with substantial metadata and an exploratory interface, not evidence that tags improve personal file retrieval. It also does not show that automatically inferred facets will be correct. Facets depend on descriptors that meaningfully describe the collection. [OR05](https://flamenco.berkeley.edu/papers/flamenco03.pdf)

**Demotion can reduce clutter without forcing deletion.** GrayArea let users move low-importance files to a less prominent part of their existing folders. The author-institution summary reports a cleaning task with 96 participants, a 13% reduction in folder clutter, and 81% finding demotion easier than deletion. This was a particular interface and short task, not a longitudinal demonstration of improved knowledge quality. It supplies a tested middle option between equally prominent retention and removal. It does not imply that demoted items are false, or that all retained items should remain eligible for current-task retrieval. [OR06](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/its-not-that-important-demoting-personal-information-of-low-subjective-importance-using-grayarea/)

**More management effort has limited and heterogeneous returns.** Englefield and Beale's paper appeared online in December 2025. Its accessible institutional abstract reports nine behavior clusters, modestly better reported retrieval for more active management, and benefits of sophisticated filing when retrieving older material for reuse. It also reports a detrimental association with deletion. This review could not inspect the full methods, effect sizes, or confounding controls. It therefore treats these as reported associations, not causal estimates, and draws no rule to retain everything or to avoid deletion. The contribution is a recent warning against a single ideal behavior profile. [OR07](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/managing-personal-information-tactics-strategies-and-outcomes/)

The 2026 HCI special-issue introduction, published online in March 2025, identifies fragmentation and AI-assisted PIM as continuing research problems. Its discussion raises a possible tradeoff: automation may save organizing effort while reducing the familiarity humans develop through active organization. This is an editorial synthesis and research agenda, not a new controlled evaluation of AI organization. Its inclusion demonstrates that the human-agent boundary remains an open question within current PIM scholarship. [OR08](https://emileerader.com/papers/whittaker_rader_bergman_PIM_special_issue.pdf)

Taken together, these studies favor investigating complementary access paths, contextual cues, and maintenance costs. They do not identify a universal winning hierarchy. Human users bring learned locations, recognition, and personal investment; agents bring different search tools and context limits. Translating a human preference directly into an agent architecture would add an untested premise.

## PARA, Johnny.Decimal, and Zettelkasten: creator practice

These frameworks answer different questions. Their original descriptions are useful design material, but they are not comparative evidence that adopting the framework improves agent outcomes.

Tiago Forte's PARA description divides information into Projects, Areas, Resources, and Archives according to its role in ongoing work. Projects have outcomes; areas continue; resources support interests; archives hold inactive material. The consulted article was published in 2023 and updated in 2026. Its simplicity and productivity claims are creator advocacy informed by practice, not controlled findings. For agents, an explicit active/inactive distinction is a plausible retrieval cue; treating the four folders as a complete authority or temporal model is this review's unsupported extension and should be avoided. [OR09](https://fortelabs.com/blog/para/)

Johnny.Decimal's current creator documentation uses areas, categories, stable numeric IDs, and an index. Its constraints aim to bound navigation choices and keep locations recognizable. This is an organizing practice, with no measured agent evaluation reviewed here; the documentation page is undated. A useful transferable idea is separating a stable identifier from a changing description. Numeric codes still require a maintained mapping, and they do not tell an agent whether a file is approved, superseded, or applicable to the current task. [OR10](https://johnnydecimal.com/documentation/introduction)

Luhmann's account of his slip box describes stable addresses, links, backlinks, and an evolving keyword register rather than a complete topical taxonomy. The English translation, cataloged by his archive as a 2015 publication of the 1981 essay, is a reflective account of one scholar's practice. His notion of communication with the collection is not an experimentally demonstrated cognitive mechanism for LLM memory. The transferable proposal is to preserve connections and bibliographic grounding while allowing concepts to change; links can also create irrelevant traversal and require upkeep. [OR11](https://luhmann.surge.sh/communicating-with-slip-boxes)

Forte's April 2026 article explicitly argues for PARA in AI-assisted work, including project-centric context and moving inactive material to archives. It describes the creator's own filing experiment and opinions about AI suitability. This is current primary practice testimony, not a controlled comparison of memory systems. Archiving is an organizational change: it becomes selective forgetting only if retrieval and context assembly actually distinguish archived material. No AI speed or accuracy improvement is established by this source for the workflows considered here. [OR12](https://fortelabs.com/blog/why-para-is-the-key-to-the-ai-era/)

A system can combine a role-based location, a stable identifier, and contextual links, but combining all three also adds maintenance. The first question is whether a concrete retrieval or update failure requires each layer. Framework adoption alone is not an outcome measure.

## Preservation and retention: mechanisms with indirect implications

**NDSA Levels 2.1 (2026)** supplies an authoritative, recent update. Its environmental sustainability guide connects preservation decisions to storage, integrity, and content work; accompanying guidance emphasizes that the highest level need not be every organization's goal in every functional area. The update concerns stewardship after selection for preservation. It is not a result about agent memory. The indirect lesson is proportionate investment with stated preservation objectives, rather than treating maximum redundancy, retention, or maturity as automatically optimal. [OR13](https://www.ndsa.org/2026/03/23/announcing-version-2_1-of-the-ndsa-levels-of-digital-preservation.html)

**OAIS, December 2024 edition**, distinguishes stored data from the representation information needed by a designated community to understand it. Its responsibilities include documented preservation policies and traceability; deletion can occur under an approved strategy. The reference model concerns archival responsibility, not a filesystem implementation recipe. An agent-facing analogy is that future readers may need units, definitions, purpose, and schema alongside a surviving file. Understandability and authenticity do not establish that a source's claims were true or remain current. [OR14](https://ccsds.org/Pubs/650x0m3.pdf)

**BagIt, RFC 8493 (2018)** defines a file-based package with manifests and checksums. Completeness and checksum verification establish its defined notion of a valid bag. Payloads are opaque bytes; paths and filenames have no semantic meaning within the format. This clean boundary matters: a valid transfer can faithfully preserve an erroneous summary or a superseded policy. BagIt can support trustworthy transfer and recovery, but cannot certify factual accuracy, source authority, confidentiality, or correct retrieval selection. [OR15](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8493.html)

**PREMIS 3.0 (2015)** defines preservation metadata around Objects, Events, Agents, and Rights. Its data dictionary records relationships and actions that help maintain and document digital objects. A lightweight agent analogy is recording a source object, the transformation that produced a summary, and the responsible software or reviewer. PREMIS's term “Agent” is broader than an LLM assistant. The standard does not prescribe a truth-validation procedure for every assertion, and recording an event does not make its outcome reliable. [OR16](https://www.loc.gov/standards/premis/v3/premis-3-0-final.pdf)

**W3C PROV-DM (2013)** is a Recommendation for representing entities, activities, agents, and relations such as derivation, revision, and invalidation. It can express that a summary came from a source or that a new entity revised an earlier one. A valid provenance description is not a valid factual claim. For agent memory, transformation lineage can make corrections auditable and expose dependence on an outdated input; deciding which assertion is authoritative still requires domain policy and evidence. [OR17](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/)

**FAIR (2016)** emphasizes identifiers, metadata, searchable registration, qualified relationships, provenance, and reuse conditions. It explicitly permits authentication and authorization and allows metadata to remain accessible after data become unavailable. FAIR principles are implementation-independent guidance, not a mandatory public-release scheme or quality certificate. Applied indirectly, they suggest making memory items addressable and describable while distinguishing discoverability from permission to read or use them. A well-indexed item can still be wrong. [OR18](https://www.nature.com/articles/sdata201618)

**TRUST (2020)** addresses repository transparency, responsibility, user focus, sustainability, and technology. Its discussion includes communicating preservation timeframes and supporting feedback on fitness for reuse. These are organizational stewardship principles, not a benchmark of answer correctness. For an agent store, the analogy is a declared scope, an accountable maintainer, and a way to report or repair problems. Reliable operations and responsible access remain separate from whether a generated answer uses evidence correctly. [OR19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41597-020-0486-7)

**DCMI Metadata Terms (2020)** offers established vocabulary for identifiers, source, provenance, modified dates, temporal coverage, versions, replacement, and access rights. It avoids inventing a vocabulary for every repository. The terms are descriptive: recording a modified date does not establish when a fact was valid, and a version relation does not identify the governing version for a particular decision. An agent store can reuse these meanings without adopting every term or confusing metadata completeness with semantic accuracy. [OR20](https://www.dublincore.org/specifications/dublin-core/dcmi-terms/)

**Memento, RFC 7089 (2013)** defines time-based access to previous Web resource states through datetime negotiation and TimeMaps. It is an Informational RFC, not an Internet Standards Track specification. Its distinction between an original resource and a frozen historical representation is relevant when source pages change. It supports answering what a resource looked like at a time; it does not resolve which historical statement was correct, whether every version was captured, or whether a past state is applicable now. [OR21](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc7089.html)

These mechanisms address different failure surfaces. Recovery preserves access; fixity detects certain byte changes; representation information supports interpretation; provenance exposes origin and transformation; descriptive metadata supports selection. Semantic checks must additionally address the claim's accuracy, effective period, authority, scope, and conflict with later evidence. Preservation and privacy controls provide important independent properties, but neither is a substitute for those checks.

## Tradeoff matrix for an agent workflow

The following is an engineering synthesis to test, not a ranking established by the cited studies. “Burden” includes the human effort to maintain the store and the agent's retrieval and context costs.

| Organizing route | Useful affordance | Burden or failure to examine | Candidate agent check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Shallow project folders | Bounds a task's working set; exposes nearby context | An item may serve several projects; moving work changes locations | Cross-project lookup and recovery after a move |
| Deep folder hierarchy | Explicit containment and fine subdivisions | Placement ambiguity and traversal; important context may sit elsewhere | Correct retrieval from unfamiliar branches |
| Free-form tags | Multiple associations without multiple copies | Inconsistent names, sparse tagging, broad matches | Precision under missing and conflicting tags |
| Keyword/full-text search | Finds exact phrases across locations | Vocabulary mismatch; old copies and repeated phrases | Known-item retrieval with obsolete distractors |
| Controlled facets | Combines explicit attributes and constrained search | Metadata assignment, schema drift, incorrect inferred values | Filter accuracy and behavior with unknown attributes |
| Stable IDs and contextual links | Addressability despite renamed descriptions; connected evidence | Index and link upkeep; unrelated traversal | Broken-link detection and correction propagation |
| Demotion or archive tier | Reduces active prominence while preserving history | Archived material may still enter ordinary search; supersession is separate | Current-state answers versus historical questions |

Folder containment can be one selection signal alongside source identity, project scope, and temporal metadata. Search can bypass a misfiled item but also bypass a useful scope boundary. Facets can clarify an ambiguous match but cannot repair invented metadata. A link graph can recover relationships without copying everything, while increasing the need to track version-specific dependencies. These are reasons to measure interactions, not reasons to deploy all mechanisms at once.

## Agent-specific hypotheses and limits

The following proposals are **workflow inferences**, not tested findings from these adjacent sources:

1. **Separate retention from eligibility.** An item may be retained for history, excluded from current context, and still discoverable on an explicitly historical query. Conversely, a recent item may be a draft that should never govern an answer. Physical location alone cannot express both distinctions reliably.
2. **Keep a concise current view with traceable support.** A project overview can identify active decisions and point to supporting versions. Its own date, scope, and maintainer matter. If it disagrees with its sources, treating the overview as automatically authoritative merely concentrates error.
3. **Record the minimum metadata needed by actual queries.** Likely candidates include stable identity, source, task scope, status, source observation time, validity period when known, and replacement links. An observation time is not automatically an effective date. Unknown values should remain unknown.
4. **Preserve transformation boundaries.** Captured material, generated summaries, proposals, verified decisions, and corrections need distinguishable roles. Repeated copying should not manufacture independent corroboration. A corrected source should trigger review of its dependent summaries where those dependencies are known.
5. **Make maintenance measurable.** Assess retrieval success, obsolete-source use, correction propagation, context size, maintenance effort, and historical reconstruction separately. Include unfamiliar projects and changed terminology; otherwise a system may appear effective only because its creator remembers the layout.

Retention should have an explicit purpose and review process. Archiving, reversible demotion, replacing a current summary, and deletion are different actions with different consequences. This review supplies no universal retention period and no jurisdiction-specific retention advice. It also supplies no basis for declaring indefinite retention best: more history can improve reconstruction while expanding the set of outdated or irrelevant items a selector must reject.

The unresolved question is empirical: which minimal combination of organization, metadata, retrieval, and maintenance improves an agent's use of changing evidence at acceptable cost? The appropriate evaluation must test current and historical questions separately, introduce contradictory versions and later corrections, and distinguish a successful file lookup from a correctly grounded answer. Human PIM and preservation provide useful mechanisms and failure models; the causal claim about agent performance remains to be demonstrated.
